Recursive reasoning models solve Sudoku-Extreme by applying a small network to a carried state, step after step, until the grid stops changing. Their reported accuracies depend on conventions that no paper states, such as how many steps are run, whether the weights are averaged, how much noise is injected and which checkpoint is selected, and these conventions move the numbers by more than the differences between the models. What the loop actually does when it solves a puzzle has not been measured. We treat the loop as a flow toward a fixed point and build a set of instruments that measure this flow directly: whether a trained model commits to cells and propagates from them or averages over possibilities, from which starting states it reaches the solution, which wrong grids it converges to, and what its confident commitments cost. We introduce the Decimating Equilibrium Cell, which keeps the block and two-timescale loop of existing models but places them on a nine-field state with every parameter shared across the digits. Relabeling the digits then relabels the output exactly, so the digit augmentation that other models rely on is not needed. Trained on the standard 1,000 puzzles without it, the cell reaches 95.1 % single-pass exact accuracy at 16 steps on the full 422,786-puzzle test set and 98.1 % at 64 steps on a 100,000-puzzle subsample, compared with 86.5 % and 93.2 % for the best publicly released model evaluated with the same code, at 4.6 times the arithmetic per step. Reading the released models and forty trained grids through the same instruments yields seven laws. The loop, not the parameter count, decides whether a model commits or averages. Every model that commits, ours included, reports full confidence on the puzzles it fails, where half of its committed cells are wrong. Selecting an answer by its convergence residual, without a verifier, works exactly when training has removed the wrong fixed points. Every training recipe eventually memorizes the thousand puzzles, so the choice of checkpoint is part of the result. Doubling the number of steps raises accuracy every time and never unsolves a solved puzzle. Exact symmetry does the work that relabeled training data does for other models, and insensitivity to the starting state can be trained in. The same instruments read the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus, which these models already solve in substantial part, as a different regime: there the answer cannot be verified, what limits a model is which rules it holds a basin for rather than how far it can search, and a failure is a confident wrong fixed point rather than an unfinished propagation. The two benchmarks that the literature treats as one family are therefore a verification game and a generalization task, and we state which instruments and mechanisms carry over between them and what the generalization task asks of the next model. Together, the results say what a recursive reasoner commits to, from which starting states it can be reached, and what its accuracy costs.

## v0 — 2026-09-08, for the professor (preliminary; Night A single-seed numbers, to be replaced by the champion night's seed triple)

**Title (fixed by the PI 2026-09-08):** Fixed Points, Basins, and the Price of Information: Measured Laws of Recursive Reasoning Models

Recursive reasoning models solve Sudoku-Extreme by applying a small network to a carried state until the grid stops changing. Their reported accuracies depend on conventions that no paper states (the number of outer steps, the averaged weights, the noise at inference, the grid selected during training) by more than the differences between the models, and the mechanism that makes the loop work has not been measured. We treat the loop as a flow toward a fixed point and build an instrument suite that measures it: whether a trained cell commits and propagates or averages, the basin from which it reaches the solution, the wrong grids it converges to, and what its commitments cost. We introduce the Decimating Equilibrium Cell, the field's block and two-timescale loop on a nine-field state with every parameter shared over the digits, so that relabeling the digits relabels the output exactly and the digit augmentation the field trains with becomes unnecessary. Trained on the standard 1,000 puzzles without it, the cell reaches 95.1 % single-pass exact accuracy at 16 outer steps on the full 422,786-puzzle test set and 98.1 % at 64 steps on a 100,000-puzzle subsample, against 86.5 % and 93.2 % for the best public checkpoint read through the same evaluator, at 4.6 times its multiply-accumulates per step. Reading the field's released checkpoints and forty trained grids through the same instruments gives seven laws: the loop, not the parameter count, decides whether a cell commits or averages; every decimating decoder, ours and the field's, states full confidence at its stalls where half of its committed cells are wrong; residual selection is verification-free exactly when training cleaned the attractors; every regime memorizes the corpus, so selection is load-bearing; depth buys accuracy at every doubling of the outer steps with no regressions; exact symmetry replaces the augmentation orbit; and invariance to the starting state is trainable. Each law names the instrument that reads it and the mechanism it constrains.

*Numbers and their sources:* 95.1 / 98.1 = A5 (DEC-w384 + FPA + RI, no digit aug, EMA, val-selected) D16 on the full test / D64 on the 100k subsample (`runs/analysis/finalA_verdict.txt`); 86.5 / 93.2 = EqR (EMA) through our evaluator, FULL test at D16 / D64 (`runs/analysis/frontier_20260907.txt`); 4.6× = the analytic MACs per outer step, DEC-w384 vs the TRM-MLP (`Report_2026-09-07_NightA_Verdict.md`, compute column); "half of its committed cells are wrong" = E3 wrong-among-committed 54–68 % on every decimating grid (`corpus_lens_20260907.txt`); "forty grids" = the corpus table's 38 grids + the two Mac field rows used by the selector law.
